# Interview guide: tsmom-etf in plain language

This is written for someone who is still learning Python and has never run a backtest. It explains what the project
does, why each piece is there, and how to answer the questions an interviewer is likely to ask. The numbers block is
regenerated by `python run.py backtest`; everything else is stable.

## The one-paragraph version

Each month, for each of ten ETFs, I ask two questions: "has this gone up over the last year?" and "is it above its
200-day average?" If both are yes I own it, up to 20% of the portfolio; otherwise I hold cash. I test that rule on 21
years of prices in a way that cannot peek at the future, charge myself realistic trading costs, compare against just
buying SPY, and then run the same rule on an Alpaca paper account with hard risk limits in front of every order.

## What is 12-1 momentum, and why skip the last month?

**Momentum** is the empirical finding that assets that have gone up over the past several months tend, on average, to
keep going up for a few more months, and vice versa. "12-1" means: measure the return from 12 months ago to 1 month
ago, and ignore the most recent month entirely.

The most recent month is skipped because at the one-month horizon prices tend to *reverse* slightly (last month's
winners are, on average, a little weaker next month). Including it adds noise that partly cancels the trend signal.
This is the standard construction in the academic literature (Jegadeesh & Titman for cross-sectional momentum;
Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen for the time-series version used here).

**Time-series** momentum means each asset is judged against *its own* past ("is SPY up?"), not ranked against the
others ("is SPY up more than TLT?"). That is why the strategy can be entirely in cash: if nothing has been going up,
nothing is held.

In code: `close.shift(21) / close.shift(252) - 1`. Twenty-one trading days is one month; 252 is one year.

## Why the 200-day moving average filter?

It is a second, slower opinion on the same question. Momentum can be positive because of one strong month a year ago
while the price has been sliding for the last six; requiring the price to be above its 200-day average means the trend is
still intact *now*. It makes the strategy slower to buy after a crash and slower to get whipsawed. It is a common
practitioner rule, not from the paper.

## Why volatility targeting?

Without it, a position in an ETF that moves 3% a day (emerging markets in 2008) carries far more risk than one that
moves 0.3% a day (7-10 year Treasuries), even at the same dollar weight. Scaling each position to a target volatility
(`weight = 10% / realised vol`) makes each position contribute a similar amount of risk, so the strategy's risk does not
lurch around depending on which assets happen to be "on".

The honest footnote, visible in the results: with a 20% cap per position and a 10% target, the scaling only reduces a
position when its volatility exceeds 50% annualised, which almost never happens at a month-end. So in this
configuration the cap does the sizing and the vol target is nearly inert. I report that rather than quietly changing the
spec. If asked "what would you change", the answer is: either raise the cap or target a *portfolio* vol instead of a
per-position vol, and re-test.

## What is look-ahead bias, and how does the test prevent it?

Look-ahead bias is using information in a decision that would not have existed at the time of the decision. The
classic accidental version: computing a signal from today's close and then "buying at today's open", or using a moving
average centred on the date instead of trailing it. It makes backtests look wonderful and live trading look nothing like
them.

Two rules in the code prevent it, and two tests prove it:

1. The signal at date *t* only uses closes up to *t* (`shift` and `rolling` are all backward-looking).
2. Trades happen at the **next day's open**, never at the close that produced the signal.

`test_no_lookahead_in_target_weights` computes the weights, then multiplies every price *after* date *t* by random
numbers (and crashes one asset to $1), recomputes, and asserts that the weights up to and including *t* are
bit-for-bit identical. If any future data leaked into the signal, the test would fail. `test_no_lookahead_in_backtest_nav`
does the same at the portfolio level: tamper with the future, and the past NAV path must not change.

## Why does out-of-sample matter?

Because you can make any rule look good on the data you designed it on. If I had tried 40 lookbacks and picked the best,
the in-sample Sharpe would be flattering and meaningless. Splitting the data at 2016 and reporting 2016-present
separately shows how the rule did on years it was not designed against.

Two subtleties I would mention:

- I did **not** fit the parameters on the in-sample period; they are the literature defaults. So the split here checks
  *stability across regimes* more than it checks overfitting.
- The out-of-sample Sharpe is actually *higher* than in-sample. That is not evidence of a great strategy; 2016-2026 was a
  long, trending bull market with two sharp momentum-friendly sell-offs (2020, 2022), and SPY's own Sharpe rose too.
  The walk-forward table is the better view: rolling 3-year windows show where the strategy is weak (it trails SPY on
  Sharpe in most windows during strong bull runs) and where it earns its keep (2008, 2022).

## "Why is your Sharpe what it is?"

Honest answer: it is a moderate-Sharpe strategy, in the same range as a 60/40 portfolio, and I did not expect otherwise.
The published time-series momentum results use futures, leverage, long *and* short positions, and dozens of markets;
diversifying across many uncorrelated trends is where the high Sharpes come from. Long-only on ten ETFs with a 100% gross
cap keeps the drawdown protection but gives up most of that diversification. The strategy's real contribution shows in
the drawdown column, not the return column: a quarter of SPY's worst drawdown, at half the volatility.

Also worth saying: Sharpe here uses a 0% risk-free rate, so all the Sharpes (benchmarks included) are slightly generous,
and 10 bps per side of costs takes a few tenths of a percent a year off the CAGR.

## "Would this work live?"

Partly. The things that transfer: the signal is slow (monthly), the ETFs are among the most liquid instruments in the
world, and turnover is low enough that costs are a drag, not a killer. The things that do not: (1) the backtest fills at
Yahoo's adjusted open price, while a real market-on-open order fills at the auction price, which can be worse in volatile
months; (2) 21 years is two or three market cycles, not a large sample, and the rolling windows overlap; (3) the
universe was chosen knowing which ETFs survived; (4) cash earns 0% in the backtest but would earn T-bill rates live,
which slightly helps. My expectation for a live version is a lower Sharpe than the backtest, similar drawdown behaviour,
and long stretches of underperforming SPY in bull markets that would test anyone's discipline. That last part is the real
reason strategies like this fail in practice.

## What the risk manager does, and why it does not flatten

Two circuit breakers halt trading: a 3% one-day loss or a 15% drawdown from the peak. On a halt the code cancels open
orders, logs the event, and refuses new orders until a human flips a flag in `settings.py`. It deliberately does **not**
sell everything, because the halt is meant to catch *my code or data doing something wrong* (a bad price feed, a bug that
sized a position 10x), and dumping a portfolio at the bottom of a real sell-off because of a limit is a way to lock in
the loss. A human looks, decides, resets.

Every order is also checked against the 20% position and 100% gross caps and the market clock before it is submitted.
The checks run on a simulated book in sequence, sells first, so a rejected order cannot accidentally make room for a
later one.

## Questions to expect, with short answers

- **Why monthly, not daily?** The signal barely changes day to day; daily rebalancing would multiply costs for no gain.
- **Why not short?** Long-only keeps the project inside what a retail cash account can do and avoids borrow costs and
  unlimited-loss risk. It also gives up a lot of the paper's diversification.
- **What would you try next?** Portfolio-level vol targeting; adding T-bill yield to cash; a wider universe (sector or
  international ETFs) to get more independent trends; testing a 10-month SMA instead of 200-day to match the monthly
  cadence.
- **What breaks the strategy?** Sharp V-shaped reversals (the strategy sells after the drop and rebuys after the
  recovery, like March-May 2020) and long choppy sideways markets (2015-2016, 2022-2023) where it gets whipsawed.
- **How do you know there is no bug?** 27 unit tests, including hand-calculated signal values, cap and scaling
  arithmetic, next-open fill timing, cost accounting, and the two look-ahead tests. And the benchmarks run through the
  same engine, so if the engine were wrong, SPY buy-and-hold would not match its known history.
